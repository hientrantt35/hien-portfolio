BEFORE YOU SCROLL

This case study isn't just about designing the ideal flow.

This case study is about designing within real business decisions, technical constraints, and a three-week launch timeline. Rather than showing every screen, I'm focusing on three moments where UX decisions had to balance user needs, business priorities, and system limitations.

My role

I owned UX design and project coordination, working directly with the client and backend team while coordinating two UI designers against the launch timeline.

Externally I led the design team: gathering requirements, hearing out the backend team on what the system could support, and presenting the outcomes to the client directly.

Internally I managed the schedule for myself and the visual designers, and followed up through each stage so the work stayed on track against the opening date.

01

Cross-Cultural UX

When a service enters a new market, assumptions about customer behavior can come with it. One example was how discounted ticket categories should be handled.

INITIAL DESIGN

I proposed removing self-declared discounts and using standard rates with in-person ID verification to reduce the risk of misuse in the Vietnam market.

THE CHALLENGE

The client chose to retain self-selection to preserve booking speed and consistency across the chain.

SOLUTION — WORKING INSIDE IT

When a business decision is fixed, the UX can still reduce ambiguity and misuse around it. So, rather than continuing to push for a structural change, I designed safeguards within the chosen model:

1. Added clearer guidelines explaining each discount category

2. Added an explicit policy reminder before users completed the selection

3. Kept the booking flow fast while making the eligibility rules more visible

SELECT RATES → CATEGORY GUIDELINES → POLICY REMINDER
02

Balancing UX with Technical Constraints

A flow that reads well on paper still has to fit the system that will run it. When the data can't take the shape the design assumes, the design is what moves.

The work is knowing which part of the experience the constraint actually costs, then paying that cost back somewhere the system doesn't control.

INITIAL DESIGN

Size and Flavor as two separate grid steps, for lower cognitive load

STEP 1 — SIZE
M
L
→
STEP 2 — FLAVOR + PRICES
THE CHALLENGE

The legacy POS/inventory system only supports a flat data structure, not nested modifiers.

Two dependent steps were not something the data behind the menu could express. And modifying the existing system wasn't an option due to the time constraint.

SOLUTION

The system constraint did not disappear. The goal was to place its complexity where it created the least friction for users.

I collapsed the two steps into one: flattened radio-button options representing pre-combined [Size + Flavor + Price] strings.

Compensated on the UX layer instead of the data layer:

1. Grouped options so the flat list still read as size-then-flavor logic visually

2. Showed price deltas transparently next to each option (e.g. +18,000đ)

3. Set the base combination as the default, so most users finish in one tap

03

Turning a feature request into an operational solution

Sometimes a feature request describes the symptom rather than the underlying problem. The client's requested "I'm here" button was one example.

So the question isn't whether to build it — it's what the request is trying to solve, and which interaction solves that without putting the outcome in the wrong hands.

CLIENT REQUEST

Add an "I'm here" button directly on the e-ticket, letting customers self-trigger the kitchen to start preparing their F&B order on arrival.

THE CHALLENGE

The interaction introduced several risks:

— Customers could trigger preparation too early

— The button could be tapped accidentally

— The system had no reliable arrival signal without introducing additional location technology

— The client was working within a tight implementation timeline

SOLUTION — WORKING INSIDE IT

Through discussion, I realized the underlying problem was not simply knowing when a customer had arrived. The client was trying to reduce long queues at the concession counter, a recurring operational issue in urban locations.

The goal was not to reproduce the requested feature. It was to solve the operational problem behind it. Rather than having customers manually trigger preparation, I proposed a more efficient concession flow:

TICKET → F&B ORDER → QR CODE → QUEUE NUMBER → PICKUP

Customers can see a QR code on their e-ticket. When they arrive, they scan the QR code to receive a queue number, which is displayed on the electronic board. They can then monitor the board and approach the counter when their number is ready. This shifted the interaction from "tell us when you arrive" to "give customers visibility into when their order is ready." It addressed the operational problem behind the request while reducing the risk of customers triggering preparation too early or accidentally.

Reflection

Three weeks, a system that was already decided, and a client who knew their business well.
